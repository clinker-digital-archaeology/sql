The Qadri (2021) article discusses how Pakistan’s National Database and Registration Authority (NADRA) encodes social and political values into technical systems. The story of Riz’s struggle to renew his Computerized National ID Card (CNIC) exemplifies how relational databases are not neutral tools as they reflect assumptions about family, citizenship, and identity. The database’s requirement that every individual be linked to two married parents privileges the nuclear, heterosexual family and marginalizes single parents, children born outside of formal marriages, or non-traditional families. In this sense, technical rules built into systems enforce social hierarchies, determining who can access essential services such as banking, healthcare, voting, and more. 
The system’s limitations extend to gender identity. Interestingly, NADRA initially used a binary gender field, with only options for ‘man or ‘woman’ genders.  However, later on in 2009, Legal advocacy forced the inclusion of the khawaja sira community, a ‘third-gender’ originating in South Asia. Yet challenges remained in representing non-biological family structures, such as in cases of orphaned children. The article discusses how the database sometimes relies on ad hoc solutions, including “database adoption” or fabrication of documentation, revealing the risks of rigid design that cannot fully accommodate social diversity.
NADRA also demonstrates how database centralization concentrates power. As the CNIC becomes a gatekeeper for civic participation, minor technical or social irregularities such missing parental records or uncertain citizenship, can block access to essential rights. This raises concerns about accountability and fairness, as administrative authority is embedded within technical systems. Riz’s experience, where months of effort were required to validate his parents’ relationship, highlights the human consequences of inflexible computational design.
The ethical challenges of NADRA underscore the broader implications for database design worldwide. Relational schemas encode assumptions that reflect cultural values and historical contingencies, shaping social inclusion or exclusion. Daily data systems, from credit scoring algorithms to social media platforms, similarly embed judgments about behavior, social norms, and identity. In these ways, technical systems can reinforce existing social inequalities and further disadvantage and alienate certain groups. 
In terms of day-to-day value systems I encounter, several examples come to mind. At the University of Toronto, the ACORN platform, which we use to register for courses and store personal data, makes implicit judgments about the information it collects. For instance, students can only enter two parents in the system, and while there is a non-binary or “prefer not to specify” option for gender, it may not be sufficient for everyone. Social media platforms also encode judgments about what content is “relevant” or “engaging,” amplifying certain voices while silencing marginalized perspectives. Credit scoring algorithms and bank loans reflect historical biases related to income, race, or neighborhood, influencing who can access loans or financial services. Even healthcare databases may exclude patients who do not fit predefined categories of gender, family, or medical history, affecting the quality of care they receive. These everyday systems, much like NADRA, demonstrate that technical tools are not neutral, and they embed assumptions that shape opportunities, access, and social inclusion. 
In conclusion, this article  suggests that ethical database design requires both technical and sociological foresight. Equally important is interdisciplinary input: involving sociologists, anthropologists, legal experts, and affected communities ensures that databases reflect lived realities rather than enforcing rigid norms. As an anthropologist, I found this argument particularly interesting. In anthropology, we often critique systems and consider how incorporating multidisciplinary perspectives can enrich our understanding and practice. This article highlighted the benefits of such an approach, which made it especially engaging for me.
